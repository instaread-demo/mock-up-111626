Oman and the New Geography of Gulf Access

By John Calabrese | Situation Reports - November 13, 2026
Updated November 16, 2026
  1. November 16, 2026 Added bio.

For decades, Oman’s location has been both an advantage and a liability. Its access to the Indian Ocean has given it a maritime reach beyond the Strait of Hormuz, while leaving it on the periphery of the Gulf’s economic boom, dominated by its larger neighbors.

The Iran war has changed the strategic value of that geography.

With traffic through Hormuz severely disrupted, Oman’s ports have become important gateways for regional trade. In May, logistics monitors identified Sohar and Duqm as primary diversion and transshipment points. On October 6, just seven commodity vessels transited the strait, while oil exports through the Gulf of Oman and Red Sea more than doubled from prewar levels. The disruption shows little sign of abating. On November 11, Iran’s security chief vowed to keep Hormuz closed.

Oman’s ports cannot absorb the volume of trade that normally passes through Hormuz or rival the region’s established logistics hubs. Their growing importance lies in giving shipping companies and Asian trading partners another way to reach the Gulf. For economies heavily dependent on Gulf energy and trade, that additional access has become a strategic consideration. China, with its extensive economic exposure to the region and limited appetite for assuming responsibility for its security, has particular reason to take notice.

From Peripheral Location to Strategic Asset

Oman has spent years trying to turn its geography into an economic asset. Its three deep-water ports, Sohar, Duqm, and Salalah, connect the country to international shipping networks. Duqm lies outside the Strait of Hormuz on the Arabian Sea. The government has long sought to make logistics a pillar of economic diversification and aspires to rank among the world’s leading logistics hubs by 2040.

Duqm is central to that ambition. Located roughly midway along Oman’s Indian Ocean coastline, it has a deep-water port capable of handling very large vessels and sits adjacent to a large special economic zone designed to attract industrial and logistics investment. Its position provides direct access to the Indian Ocean and proximity to markets in Asia, Africa, and the Gulf.

The infrastructure predates the war. The crisis has changed the commercial and strategic calculations surrounding it. The disruption of Hormuz has exposed how much the value of a port depends on the maritime routes ships must use to reach it. Duqm has a clear geographic advantage because it faces the Arabian Sea. Sohar also lies outside the strait, although closer to it, while Salalah remains exposed to instability around the Bab el-Mandeb. The latter has already experienced the effects of regional insecurity.

Duqm’s location alone will not make it a substitute for Jebel Ali. Cargo must be supported by regular shipping services, warehouses, trucking networks, efficient customs procedures, industrial customers, and dependable connections to inland markets. Companies will use a port consistently when it offers the capacity, cost, and reliability their supply chains require.

The war is giving Oman an opportunity to build that commercial foundation. Its ports have largely continued operating while traffic through Hormuz has fluctuated, and Muscat has been exploring additional logistics links with neighboring countries. In early September, a Saudi delegation visited Sohar, Duqm, and Salalah to examine their capabilities and opportunities for investment and logistics cooperation. Saudi crude is already moving through Omani waters, with Aramco offering additional loadings to Asian refiners out of Sohar.

Oman and Egypt have also discussed a direct shipping route linking Duqm and Salalah with Egyptian ports. The proposed connection is intended to support industrial value chains and improve access to African and Asian markets. The participating ports and operators, along with a launch date, have yet to be announced.

Taken together, these initiatives point to a wider regional interest in developing additional routes for moving goods and energy out of the Gulf. Oman stands to benefit if it can turn that interest into regular commercial traffic.

China’s Interest in Access and Security

China’s position in the Gulf helps explain why this matters.

Over two decades, Beijing has built a substantial economic presence in the region, spanning energy, infrastructure, manufacturing, technology, and logistics. Its exposure has grown alongside its commercial reach. The Middle East supplied roughly half of China’s crude oil imports in 2025, leaving its energy security closely tied to maritime routes that Beijing has shown little inclination to protect militarily.

The Iran war has brought the costs of that dependence into sharper focus. Chinese shipping behavior offers an early indication of the adjustment underway. Since late July, state-controlled COSCO Shipping Energy Transportation and China Merchants Energy Shipping, which handled roughly half of China’s Middle East crude imports before the war, have kept their tankers out of Hormuz and Bab el-Mandeb. Meanwhile, ship-to-ship transfers involving Chinese- and Hong Kong-owned vessels in the Gulf of Oman exceeded 600,000 barrels per day in June and July. The pattern has held into the autumn. Aramco invited Chinese refiners to bid for late-October cargoes on a ship-to-ship basis near Sohar.

These developments point to a search for greater flexibility in accessing Gulf supplies. Oman offers one means of pursuing it. Beijing can expand commercial ties with Omani ports, industrial zones, and logistics networks while leaving the security of Hormuz largely to others. Oman is also an established oil supplier, accounting for about 6 percent of China’s crude imports in 2025.

The relationship received a high-level boost during Sultan Haitham bin Tarik’s early November visit to Beijing, the first by an Omani head of state since diplomatic relations were established in 1978. The two sides signed 14 cooperation agreements, covering AI, green energy, and supply chains, alongside a currency swap agreement. A further 23 commercial and investment agreements followed.

The publicly listed memoranda covered space, navigation, digital currencies, and industrial supply chains. They included no agreements on defense or maritime security. The joint statement called for the resumption of safe passage through Hormuz and renewed U.S.-Iranian talks.

That pattern is consistent with Beijing’s broader approach to the Gulf. China seeks to expand its economic influence while avoiding the security commitments and military entanglements associated with the American role in the region. Oman can help reduce some of the vulnerabilities created by this approach while allowing Beijing to maintain its existing posture.

An Asian Interest Extending beyond China

China’s interest should not obscure the wider significance of Oman’s location. India has equally strong reasons to value Omani connectivity. The India-Oman Comprehensive Economic Partnership Agreement entered into force in June, while Sohar, Duqm, and Salalah offer important links between India, the Gulf, and East Africa. Omani shipping networks already connect these ports directly with Mundra, Nhava Sheva, and Hazira.

Japan and South Korea have comparable concerns. The Middle East supplied 94 percent of Japan’s crude imports and 69 percent of South Korea’s in 2025. Both have reason to diversify their routes as well as their sources of supply. The adjustment is already visible off Sohar, where two Qatari LNG cargoes exiting Hormuz in October conducted ship-to-ship transfers, one bound for South Korea and the other for Pakistan.

Oman’s opportunity therefore extends beyond its relationship with Beijing. Its ports and shipping connections could serve several Asian economies with different commercial interests and strategic relationships. That would reinforce Muscat’s longstanding preference for maintaining ties across competing political camps, from Iran and the GCC to the United States, India, and China. A more diversified logistics network would give practical expression to that diplomatic approach.

The Limits of Oman’s Opportunity

Oman’s prospects should be assessed against the considerable obstacles it faces. Alternative infrastructure has to operate at sufficient scale and competitive cost to attract sustained business. The country’s ports are still some distance from matching the capacity and commercial ecosystems of the established regional hubs. Duqm, in particular, needs a larger industrial base and more regular cargo traffic to turn its geographic advantage into a durable commercial position. Maintaining infrastructure also requires a steady flow of business to justify the investment.

The security environment presents another challenge. In March, drones struck fuel storage tanks at Duqm and Salalah, and Salalah suspended terminal operations for a time. Iran denied involvement in the Salalah attack. Salalah’s position on the Arabian Sea gives it an advantage over ports inside the Gulf, although it remains exposed to instability around the Red Sea and Bab el-Mandeb, where Houthi forces have seized territory up to the strait itself.

Duqm avoids the immediate risks associated with Hormuz, but its shipping connections remain vulnerable to a wider maritime conflict. On November 7, U.S. forces disabled a St. Kitts-flagged cargo ship in the Gulf of Oman. U.S. Central Command said the vessel had been trying to breach the blockade of Iran. The incident underscored the extent to which insecurity can reach waters beyond the strait itself.

Muscat’s diplomatic balancing act faces its own pressures. Oman has spent months negotiating with Tehran on a joint mechanism for managing traffic through Hormuz. On November 5, the Omani and Iranian foreign ministers stressed the need for practical arrangements and joint technical mechanisms to end the state of war. Iran has made U.S. recognition of a bilateral Iran-Oman arrangement for Hormuz one of its conditions for normalizing transit. President Donald Trump, meanwhile, has threatened to bomb Oman if it gets in the way of a U.S.-Iran deal. Muscat’s ability to remain a useful interlocutor for governments that distrust one another is under considerable strain.

The prospect of new energy infrastructure raises further questions. A pipeline terminating in Oman could give Gulf producers another outlet to the Indian Ocean. TotalEnergies’ chief executive said in late September that Saudi Arabia had begun work on a pipeline to Duqm. Rystad Energy, however, understands the project to be at the feasibility stage, and Aramco declined to comment. Oman’s state energy company OQ plans to double onshore oil storage at Duqm to 10 million barrels within three years.

Such projects could strengthen Oman’s position, although their cost would be substantial. Pipelines and storage facilities would also create valuable fixed assets exposed to attack in a future conflict. Gulf producers have other options. The UAE says a second bypass pipeline to Fujairah is nearly half complete, building on infrastructure that already allows some oil exports to avoid Hormuz.

For Asian importers, the case for diversifying access to the Gulf is likely to endure beyond the current crisis. Their response may involve a broader portfolio of routes, including Gulf pipelines, Omani ports, Indian Ocean shipping, Red Sea corridors, and overland connections. Oman could become a more important part of that network, provided it can attract the investment and commercial traffic needed to sustain it.

A Different Source of Strategic Importance

For much of the modern history of the Gulf, strategic geography has been associated with control over oil fields, ports, pipelines, and maritime chokepoints. Oman’s importance may increasingly derive from its position within a more diversified system of access.

The country lacks the economic scale of Saudi Arabia and the logistics infrastructure of the UAE. It cannot guarantee the security of Asian energy supplies, and Hormuz will remain indispensable to regional trade. Its ports nevertheless give shipping companies and trading partners additional choices when established routes become difficult or dangerous to use.

That matters particularly to China. The deeper its economic integration into the Gulf becomes, the greater its exposure to disruptions in the region. Beijing has shown little inclination to assume the security commitments required to protect every route on which its interests depend. Closer commercial ties with Oman offer one way to reduce that exposure without drawing China into a larger military role.

India, Japan, and South Korea have their own reasons to develop more dependable access to Gulf markets and energy supplies. Their involvement would also give Oman a broader commercial base and help preserve the flexibility of its foreign policy.

The Gulf’s established logistics hubs will retain their dominance, and Hormuz will remain a vital artery for regional trade. The war has nevertheless raised the value of locations that can keep goods and energy moving when the main routes are disrupted. Oman has spent years trying to translate its geography into an economic advantage. The crisis has given that effort a more immediate strategic purpose. Its success will depend on whether Muscat can turn a temporary demand for alternative access into lasting commercial relationships and infrastructure.

 

Dr. John Calabrese teaches international relations at American University and serves as a non-resident senior fellow at the Middle East Institute (MEI).
