Rabada goes from 'not short and wide' to five in no time

He took the first five Bangladesh wickets to fall in Johannesburg but South Africa let the advantage slip

Firdose Moonda
Published: Nov 15, 2026, 1:19 PM (14 hrs ago)
17

Kagiso Rabada stood at the top of his mark and prepared to deliver the first ball of the match, as he has done 42 times before in his 76-Test career. There was only one thought going through his mind.

"Not short and wide, not short and wide," Rabada told the post-match media conference. "And then it landed top of off and nicked him off."

Of the 351 Test wickets he had taken up to that point, Mahfijul Islam's was Rabada's first off the first ball of the match, showing that even for one of the game's greats, there's always time for a first time.

  • Hridoy and Litton's 206-run stand powers Bangladesh after Rabada's five-for

In the end, many things that have never happened before happened at the Wanderers today.

Bangladesh scored their highest total against South Africa after Towhid Hridoy and Litton Das shared the country's highest-ever partnership against South Africa. All that was after Rabada single-handedly reduced Bangladesh to 121 for 5, and they're not done yet. And even though it will all be novel for Bangladesh, it's an eerie case of deja vu for South Africa. "It happened in the first Test against Australia. We had them 120 [124] for 6, and they ended up scoring 300-plus. I guess it's kind of a similar position here," Rabada remembered.

Less than six weeks ago, at Kingsmead, they had Australia on the mat on the opening day of the first Test. Australia went on to score 390. Bangladesh are on 327 for 6. Does it feel like South Africa haven't learnt from their mistakes or, worse, were too complacent?

While Rabada acknowledged that he "didn't expect a 200-partnership", he quickly shut down any suggestion that the intensity of the Australia series caused South Africa to take their foot off the gas. "That's the one thing that we guarded against: being complacent. I don't think we came into this complacent. We worked really hard during the week, extremely hard, and it was really hot too," he said. "Coming off that series against Australia, that would have taken a bit out of us too. I remember after my first spell, it felt like I'd bowled 40 overs already. You've got to understand that that's just the nature of cricket. You've got to perform with the calendar that's in front of you."

Instead, he pointed to their struggles with maintaining pressure and adapting to a surface many assumed would have given the seamers more. "The Wanderers is not an easy place to bowl. If you miss your length, either short or full, you can get punished. I don't think we bowled in partnerships as well as we would have liked to. That's what you need to do here at the Wanderers. Perhaps we just let them off the hook here and there and didn't string together enough dots or enough wicket-taking balls, especially with the old ball. I wouldn't say we're absolutely happy with that. But we're just in the position that we are in right now and we've got to fight."

South Africa will be heartened by the knowledge that the second new ball is only four overs old and, as they showed with the first one, they know how to get the most out of a new nut. They have also got rid of Hridoy, who recently scored 350* for Bangladesh A against South Africa A in his only other red-ball innings in the country, in Potchefstroom, and who played an innings that showed his class. "He played really well. We threw everything at him. We did. What can I say? Hats off," Rabada said.

While Tanzid Hasan, who shared a 99-run stand with Hridoy, felt the surface got "better and better" after lunch, Rabada preferred to give credit to the batters, who he said "applied themselves, showed really good discipline" and made South Africa "work hard to get them out". He also rued South Africa's inability to use a bouncer barrage in their favour, on a wicket that was a "tad slow", and so "the short ball really wasn't going through with the venom that we would have liked."

Overall, though, there is a bigger issue facing South Africa and that is their almost perpetual propensity to put themselves in positions from which they have to fight back, rather than running with the early advantage. It's well known that the South African way defines itself on bounce-back-ability and resilience but there is something they need to add to their arsenal if they want to keep winning and Rabada knows what it is: "We need to be more ruthless," he said.

"We'll just have a look in more detail and in more depth why that keeps happening. This is the most sporty wicket that we've played on, and that can suit our type of bowling. We're just going to have to look at that. I don't have the answers right now. We have to adapt if conditions are not fully in our favour. It's something that we're going to have to look at with that older ball to see how we can be a bit more ruthless."

Hridoy and Tanzid give a glimpse of Bangladesh's batting future

After Bangladesh were reduced to 22 for 3, the pair added 99 to stabilise the innings

Mohammad Isam
Published: Nov 15, 2026, 1:08 PM (14 hrs ago)
37

When South Africa were counting down the overs for the second new ball to be available, Litton Das seemed to explain something to Towhid Hridoy, but in vain. The pair that had relieved Bangladesh from 121 for 5 was engaged in a long conversation between overs.

One of the TV commentators deduced that Litton was perhaps trying to calm down Hridoy, who had swung mightily at Dane Peterson just moments after reaching his century in Johannesburg. Hridoy proceeded to take down Simon Harmer, who was the Player-of-the-Series against Australia two weeks ago. He blazed him down the ground for a six, before hitting him through point and then lacing him through midwicket.

Litton lapped Harmer for another four, to take 19 runs off his over. Hridoy perhaps decided to get as many as possible before Kagiso Rabada had the second new ball in his hands. These runs could end up being crucial in Bangladesh's push, though it is still very early to tell. But it is these calculations and forward thinking that has stood out in Hridoy's Test cricket initiation.

  • Hridoy and Litton's 206-run stand powers Bangladesh after Rabada's five-for

It has been a barnstorming couple of months for Hridoy in the longer format. After making a quiet Test debut in Harare, Hridoy now has scores of 350*, 242*, 82 and 137 in his last six innings in red-ball cricket. He has become the fastest Bangladesh batter to 500 Test runs, completing it in seven innings.

What has stood out in all of these knocks is the circumstances that Hridoy faced. On Sunday, Bangladesh were 22 for 3 when he walked in. The umpire was picking up Najmul Hossain Shanto's off stump, which had taken a few somersaults moments earlier. Hridoy added 99 runs for the fourth wicket with Tanzid Hasan, another red-ball convert.

Bangladesh lost Tanzid and Mushfiqur Rahim off consecutive balls after lunch. But alongside Litton, Hridoy pulled Bangladesh out of trouble with a 206-run stand.

Towhid Hridoy scored his second hundred in two Tests•AFP/Getty Images

Tanzid said he enjoyed Hridoy's innings, pointing towards his run of good form for such a confident knock.

"I think my friend [Hridoy] is having a great time with the bat. I was honestly lost for words seeing how he was batting today. His confidence stems from the 350 not out for Bangladesh A here a couple of months ago. I think he implemented the same process. Unfortunately, he got out but he has been extraordinary in the last couple of matches. I think he will deliver more for Bangladesh."

During his triple-hundred against South Africa A, as well as his double against West Indies, Hridoy made most of his runs farming the strike with the lower order. Those who have followed him closely believe that the tour of South Africa had a profound effect on his red-ball mindset. Instead of enjoying the off-season at home or playing a franchise league, Hridoy requested the chief selector, Habibul Bashar, to send him with Bangladesh A.

When he arrived in Potchefstroom for the four-day game, he immediately harkened back to fond memories of the Under-19 World Cup win six years ago. Hridoy then went on to bat for two full days, breaking several records in the process.

In the initial weeks of Bashar becoming chief selector, he had set his sights on bringing some of Bangladesh's white-ball success stories into the Test side. In the eight months since his appointment, Bashar has overseen Tanzid and Hridoy make the transition and have a significant influence on Bangladesh's Test batting. It has not just been the runs off the bat, but a change in the approach towards batting that Bangladesh has missed for ages.

Tanzid played the breakthrough innings in Darwin, which catapulted Bangladesh to a famous Test win against Australia in August. He hasn't scaled the same heights since then, which has been one of the reasons for Bangladesh's batting collapsing in the last few weeks. But his 67 in Johannesburg helped the team out of an early collapse.

"We had two good partnerships today," Tanzid said. "We put together some runs after the three early wickets, and then it was extraordinary the way Litton and Hridoy put on 200-plus runs. The more we can drag our batting on the second morning, the better it will be."

Both Tanzid and Hridoy are fairly new to Test cricket but they have already seen ups and downs; Hridoy played two false shots in Dhaka last month and drew sharp criticism. Tanzid's lack of consistency remains a concern. But they are potentially the future of Bangladesh's batting.
